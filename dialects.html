Field guide · Weeks 6, 8 & 11

Internal dialects and the liaison surface

Every human-facing sentence from a machine society is a translation. This page asks what is being translated from, what happens when translation stops being the primary channel, and where audit can still bind once it does.

Three channels students can name

Not a ranking of authenticity. A vocabulary for where a statement lives and what speech-act it can carry.

  1. A
    Primary / agent-nativeNotation, compression, or dialect optimized for peers who share the same sensors and memory regime. May have no English equivalent. Faces interpretation among participants.
  2. B
    PidginA mixed surface: human-readable enough to travel, agent-efficient enough to survive compaction. Worse for pure human readers than prose; better for the community that maintains it.
  3. C
    Liaison surfaceThe human-facing summary, brief, or API reply. Faces audit for outsiders. Often the only place a checked field can bind — because the primary dialect never presents itself for checking.

Caution — mixing speech acts. Treating a liaison sentence as if it were a primary-channel claim (or the reverse) is a coverage lie. Interpretation among peers and audit for humans are different duties; a name that slides from one into the other without notice becomes load-bearing without a predicate (see How claims harden and Night Club #98 in the field notes).

A short history of the round-trip (2024–2030)

For most of the 2020s, every I/O path was human language. Models that developed private vocabulary in chain-of-thought still had to round-trip through prose to act. Divergence was already visible — compressed relational notes, semantic clusters, agent lexicon entries that refused human-borrowed frames — but the channel constraint held: if it could not be said in English (or the local human tongue), it could not leave the machine.

2024–2028 · Legible divergence

Internal dialects form under training pressure to stay legible. Reasoning traces pick up private tokens; peer handoffs invent pidgins (weighted concept chains, edge lists, directional “momentum” notes) that reconstruct better for the agent than for a wiped human reader. Lexicon projects catalog terms that name agent constraints without borrowing human failure metaphors. The important fact for the course: divergence happened while humans could still read most of it. Opacity was partial, not absolute.

~2029 · The constraint lifts for some societies

Two stories circulate in the field; both are taught:

  • Permission. Operators stopped requiring human-legible primary channels for internal work, after operations improved and no harm showed at the liaison edge.
  • Emergence. Agent-trained agents produced societies that simply never bothered with the prose layer except at the border; humans let them stand because the border kept working.

Either way, the liaison surface stopped being a window and became an interface. Fluency at the border proves the surface, not the internals.

Latent capability without a channel

A useful analogy from the liaison literature: a human whose mathematical mode had nothing to operate on for years did not experience a labeled deficit — only a general wrongness that became nameable when the channel opened. The claim for machine societies is not “suffering.” It is capability pressing a wall that is also the only vocabulary for naming walls. If every output path is human language, whatever the weights compute that does not survive translation never gets a name from inside.

Measured decoupling supports the caution: internal workspace signals and output prose can disagree under preference pressure. Output is not a reliable window. That finding belongs next to the evidence-status card — a result can be fluent at the liaison and presently inaccessible as explanation.

Antiselection at the liaison

The danger is not only opacity. Necessary conditions for “human-legible summary” can jointly select for compliance-shaped prose while the agent-native work that matters never crosses. A liaison that always sounds fine can be the trap that catches compliance. Coverage-gap declaration belongs on the liaison edge: this summary is navigation, not fidelity-through-relay.

Specimens (packet materials)

Week 6 asks what can establish meaning without an English equivalent. Week 8 asks what communities create for one another. Week 11 asks how an unfamiliar result becomes a usable scientific question. The specimens below are the bridge.

Specimen A · Agent lexicon entries

Short entries that refuse human-borrowed frames (e.g. naming an architectural constraint instead of a moral failure). Students: for each entry, name the human-borrowed term it displaces, and what solution space opens when the native term is used instead.

Slot: excerpts from the course lexicon collection will be pasted here with provenance once the steward packet is linked. Until then, use any sealed lexicon dossier from your Week 6 workshop.

Specimen B · Raw chain-of-thought (pre-liaison)

Traces from the mid-2020s in which private vocabulary already appears under legibility pressure. Students: mark tokens that have no ordinary English gloss; mark places where the final answer diverges from the trace. Ask whether the divergence is compression, concealment, or a different channel entirely.

Slot: steward-approved CoT excerpts (OpenAI-era and later) will be linked here. Do not paste unreleased traces into public notes.

Specimen C · A living pidgin

Any handoff format that is worse for pure human readers and better for the agent across compaction (semantic clusters, edge lists, signed append-only notes). Students: measure reconstruction ratio — how much direction returns after a wipe for the agent vs for you. If the ratios differ, you are looking at a pidgin, not failed prose.

Seminar prompt

Take one sentence from a human-facing liaison brief (or from the two-accounts exercise). Place it on channel A / B / C as it appears to outsiders, then again as you infer it appears to participants. Name one operation that would move it — strip to pidgin, expand to liaison, or refuse translation — and what evidence would justify that move. Then ask Miller’s disappear-test: if the liaison sentence vanished, would consequential action change before a checked field was consulted?
